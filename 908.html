01 / PCB design · Mechanical integration · Software

Vacuum-pump
burn-in station redesign.

I redesigned a legacy pump carrier board for an updated P2 vacuum pump and developed a Python application to record and display telemetry from the burn-in rack.

Problem statement

The original burn-in rack was built around an earlier P2 pump. The newer pump used different connectors and mounting holes. An interim adapter made it possible to connect the motor, but the pump faced backward, the mount was insecure, and the heater and thermistor connections were still incompatible. The rack needed an electrical and mechanical update, along with a way to collect pump data.

My contribution

I redesigned the carrier board, including migration of the legacy OrCAD design to Altium, component selection, schematic capture, and PCB layout. I also modified microcontroller firmware, assembled custom RS-485 cables, and developed the Python monitoring application.

Carrier-board electrical design

I updated the pump connectors, brought the design into the company’s component library, and improved schematic readability. Circuit changes included a revised switch-control circuit with debouncing, removal of a second GPIO expander, and low-current indicator LEDs to simplify the bill of materials.

In the layout, I replaced the 5 V and 3.3 V planes with traces to make room for a second ground plane. I added stitching vias, shortened I²C routing and kept it on one layer, and increased separation between PWM lines to reduce coupling. I also revised routing and placement with manufacturability in mind.

Pump-mount redesign

The redesigned mounting scheme oriented the pump forward, restrained it more securely, and allowed tool-less attachment to the carrier board. This also made the pump accessible for an RPM measurement.

RS-485 data acquisition and monitoring

The rack’s main boards already supported full-duplex RS-485. After correcting firmware bugs, I obtained motor current and voltage, heater current and voltage, and pump-temperature readings from the carrier electronics. I designed and assembled an RS-485 harness to support 20 devices and fit the existing station.

I then developed a Python application that recorded data from 60 pumps simultaneously and displayed live telemetry. Its interface provided a rack overview and individual pump views, making the measurements easier to follow during a burn-in run.

Results

Carrier-board, mount, and monitoring updates.

The project combined a redesigned carrier PCB and pump mount with RS-485 data acquisition and a Python monitoring application. Connector selection, mechanical access, firmware behavior, and measurement requirements informed the system design.

02 / Signal integrity · Board rework · Debugging

USB 3.0
signal-path investigation.

An unreliable SuperSpeed connection required a systematic investigation of the signal path. I used board rework to isolate components and help identify the capacitance contributing to the failure.

Problem statement

The PMSB board could establish USB 2.0 communication but did not reliably connect at USB 3.0 speeds. A layout revision and trials with different common-mode chokes had not resolved the issue. The investigation focused on the SuperSpeed path from the system-on-module to the USB-C port, including the mux, protection components, connectors, and cable.

System-on-module→High-speed mux→Chokes & ESD protection→FFC & USB-C port

Signal-path isolation

I soldered a USB Type-A cable into different points along the path and set the direction, ID, and power signals to support the test connection. Starting from a reduced path, I reintroduced circuit elements one at a time and checked where USB 3.0 connectivity disappeared.

ESD diode capacitance

USB 3.0 connectivity was lost when the original ESD protection diodes were reintroduced. Those diodes were rated at 0.5 pF. Replacing them with 0.28 pF parts restored connectivity in the tested configuration. The investigation indicated that the diodes contributed to the path’s total capacitive loading; they were not the only element affecting signal integrity.

Documented result

USB 3.0 connectivity restored.

The final configuration connected to a host or device with passive cables tested up to 3 m. The next board revision incorporated the lower-capacitance diodes, a revised footprint intended to reduce crosstalk, and solder-paste mask updates along the USB path.

The investigation used a reduced, functional signal path as a baseline. Components were reintroduced individually to identify changes associated with the connection failure.

03 / Controller diagnostics · Design for assembly

USB-C water-detection
fault analysis.

Ten PMSB boards could not detect a USB stick. Reading the port-controller registers revealed a water-detection shutdown, even though the port was dry.

Controller diagnostics

The controller uses water-detection circuitry to check whether a monitored line responds correctly as it is driven high and low. A distorted response can trigger a protective shutdown. During this investigation, the detection lines still appeared faulty with the USB port disconnected, moving the focus away from the connector and toward the board circuitry.

Assembly-related fault investigation

The investigation pointed to the small FETs driving the detection lines. Their closely spaced, small pads were difficult to assemble reliably, creating an opportunity for soldering faults that affected the detection signals. My work included reading controller registers and using custom firmware and serial commands to investigate USB-C/PD controller operation.

Revision direction

Proposed FET package revision.

The planned Rev A design replaced the small FETs with leaded dual-FET packages for more reliable assembly. At the time of the internship presentation, water detection was disabled in prototype units. The slides document the revision approach rather than a completed production validation.

Controller diagnostics and inspection of the component assembly informed the fault investigation and the proposed package revision.
