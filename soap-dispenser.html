01 / Project requirements

Touchless dispensing
for workshop use.

Students and staff in Boston University’s EPIC workshop often have oil or debris on their hands. The project objective was to dispense soap without requiring contact with the bottle or pump.

System operation

The user places a hand between two sensor arms beneath the soap nozzle. Breaking the IR beam triggers the Arduino-controlled actuator to depress the bottle’s pump. An H-bridge reverses the actuator’s polarity so it can retract and reset.

Hand breaks beam→Arduino reads sensor→H-bridge drives actuator→Pump dispenses & resets

My contribution

During fabrication, I primarily designed the acrylic housing in Onshape and revised the panel joints and component alignment to accommodate the actuator load. During final integration, I performed most of the debugging of the housing, existing control code, and wiring.

The original control code was written by teammates. The sensing and actuation system, fabrication, and validation were part of the team’s overall project; my software contribution was debugging the existing code during final integration.

02 / CAD · Fabrication · Design iteration

Housing design
and load support.

The enclosure supported the actuator’s reaction force and maintained alignment between the pump, actuator, and sensor arms.

Panel-joint design

The team selected laser-cut acrylic for fabrication and cleaning. Conventional finger joints could separate under loading along the joint direction. I developed a custom T-finger-joint housing design to improve panel restraint under the actuator load.

The team built three housing prototypes. Revisions addressed joint clearances, panel fit, component alignment, and support under actuator loading.

Actuator and pump alignment

The team used a 3D-printed actuator mount and a separate printed interface fitted to the soap-pump cap. These parts transferred the actuator’s motion to the pump, while the acrylic enclosure provided support and clearance. My housing work had to accommodate those interfaces without allowing the pump or actuator to shift out of alignment.

03 / Final integration

System integration
and debugging.

During final integration, I resolved most of the remaining issues involving the housing, existing control code, and wiring.

Mechanical, electrical, and control behavior

The system depended on the sensor, Arduino, H-bridge, and linear actuator working together. The housing had to hold the actuator in position, the wiring had to carry power and control signals correctly, and the code had to drive the actuator through a dispense-and-reset cycle.

I investigated dispensing failures by checking mechanical alignment, electrical connections, and control behavior in the assembled prototype. This work included troubleshooting the existing code written by teammates.

Integration results

Integrated prototype operation.

Panel fit, actuator alignment, wiring, and control timing affected dispensing performance. Repeated testing of the integrated prototype identified a remaining actuator-force limitation.

Sensor response and dispensing time

The sensor response was described as nearly instantaneous and too quick to measure precisely by hand. The full time from inserting a hand to dispensing soap averaged about 2.5 seconds over ten trials. These are different measurements: the report’s under-two-second target applied to detection, not the complete mechanical cycle.

Limitation & next iteration

Actuator-force limitation.

During testing, the soap pump became stiffer and dispensing failures increased. The team identified a stronger linear actuator as the next improvement to investigate. That change was proposed rather than validated, so the measured reliability remains 87% for this prototype.

Repeated testing identified a mechanical-load limitation that was not evident from enclosure fit or sensor response alone. Further validation would be required after any actuator change.
